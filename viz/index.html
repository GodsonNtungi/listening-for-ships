Listening for ships in a noisy sea

1 · The sea is never quiet

Every sound has its own pitch range

A hydrophone does not hear a ship on its own. It hears the whole sea at once. Wind and breaking waves make a steady hiss across most pitches. Snapping shrimp crackle at the top. Whales sing in the middle. A ship's engine and propeller rumble low.

The challenge data never gives you the audio itself. It gives you how much energy landed in each of 25 pitch bands, from deep rumble to high crackle. Turn the sources on and off to see how they stack up.

2 · What you actually get

Two hours of sea, as a grid of numbers

Each minute, the 25 bands become one column of the grid. A passing ship leaves a shape you learn to spot: a low, dark swell that grows as it approaches and fades as it leaves.

You also get ship-tracking (AIS) positions: where nearby ships said they were. But a ship being nearby does not prove it made the sound. Distant ships fade into the background. Small boats often carry no tracker at all.

3 · Nobody labelled the real data

So the organisers built a test they can grade

No one has gone through the South African recordings and marked "ship here, ship not there". With no answer key, there is nothing to score against.

So each test window is made like a cocktail: take a real stretch of background, then mix in a recorded ship passing at a known loudness. Because they did the mixing, they know exactly how much of the energy came from the added ship. Some windows get no ship at all.

4 · The number, exactly

A share of energy, not a yes or no

Add up the ship's energy in every minute and every band. Divide by the total energy in the mixed window. If the ship added 2 units to a window holding 10, the answer is 0.20. If nothing was added, it is 0, even if the background has ships of its own.

One trap: sound is usually measured in decibels, a log scale, but the share is computed on raw energy. In decibels, a ship just 10 dB quieter than the background sounds close. In energy it is only a sliver. Drag the dot.

Loud minutes count far more than quiet ones. One minute at the ship's closest pass can outweigh the whole rest of the window.

5 · How you're scored

One minus your typical miss

For each test window you submit one share. Zindi measures how far off you are, on average, with bigger misses punished more. The score is 1 minus that typical miss, so 1.0 is perfect.

The organisers' starter model scores 0.754: it misses by about 0.25 on a typical window. That is the bar to beat. Below, every dot is a test window. Slide your error down and watch the dots snap to the line.

6 · Our first move

Make our own answer key

If the organisers can mix a known ship into real background, so can we. Take the unlabelled South African background, mix in ship recordings from a free public library (VTUAD, from Ocean Networks Canada), and we get as many practice windows as we like, each with a known answer.

Then teach a model to read the shape of a passing ship: how much the low bands swell, how the swell rises and falls over the minutes, how it stands out from the window's own normal level.

7 · The clock and the rules

Eight weeks, ten tries a day

  1. Test windows are for predicting only. No training, tuning or adjusting on them, and no reverse-engineering answers from the leaderboard.
  2. Outside data is allowed if it is public. Declare it on the discussion board with links and versions so everyone can get it.
  3. Open-source tools only, no auto-ML. Pretrained models are fine if anyone can download them.
  4. Pick 2 final submissions. Only those count on the hidden 70%. Small public-board gaps are noise.
  5. Top 10 hand in code within 48 hours. It must rerun to the same score, so fix every random seed from day one.
